Check it before you use it

This is the part that matters. The measurement can fail quietly: if the two families of trajectories are misread, a number still comes out and still looks plausible. So look at the overlay. Each mark is one trajectory crossing a vertical cut — red for one family, blue for the other — and every number in the histogram is the gap between two marks of the same colour on the same cut. The marks should lie along the soot tracks and string out into lines following them. This is the measurement itself, not a drawing of it.

How it measures

A soot foil carries two families of triple-point trajectories, and the cell width is the transverse distance between adjacent trajectories of one family. Two traps make this easy to get wrong, silently. The pattern is usually sheared, so the trajectory bounding a cell transversely is not the one directly above it, and a nearest-neighbour rule invents a tail of wide cells. And the cells group into larger patches, so the dominant periodicity of the raw image — what autocorrelation or a spectrum returns — is the grouping, not the cell. On one experimental plate that error was a factor of three.

SootViz separates the families first, with a bank of oriented line filters, fits their angles per block so the pattern may turn along a long record, and measures each family on its own. The two families are then independent measurements of the same quantity, so their disagreement is an error bar, reported with every result alongside an orientation coherence.

Lighting, grain and faint contrast cost essentially nothing on their own — that is what the cleaning stage is for. Only genuine irregularity moves the answer, and only every defect at once costs more than a few per cent. Note that alignment stays near 90 throughout: even where the answer drifts, the measurement is still following the record rather than inventing structure.

Published foils — measured from the authors' own figures

Taileb, Melguizo-Gavilanes & Chinnayya, Combustion and Flame 218 (2020) 247, Fig. 5. The authors report a range over their three chemistry models rather than a value per panel — “the cell size was numerically estimated to lie in the range of 0.9 mm ≤ λnum ≤ 1.9 mm” (p. 17) — and state that detailed chemistry gives the smaller cell, while single-step was tuned to the experimental size. So what can honestly be checked is range containment and that ordering:

Real records

A numerical foil from our own solver, 30 × 20 mm: λ = 2.39 mm, IQR 1.79–3.10, families agree to 11 %, alignment 90. A photographed experimental plate, 5 m × 1 m, where the pattern turns along the record: λ = 0.181 m over 13 blocks, with family angles swinging from +35°…+65° to −65°…−45°. There the families agree to 2 % and the crossings sit at the 83rd percentile of ridge strength, but the scale sensitivity is 23 % — that record genuinely has no single cell width, and its histogram is visibly bimodal. Measuring the same plate from a 16× larger scan gives 0.184 m: the analysis always happens at the same cells-per-pixel, so the answer does not depend on how finely a record was scanned.
